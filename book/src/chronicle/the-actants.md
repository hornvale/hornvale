# The Actants

The question that started this was a maintenance question, asked the way you
ask whether the filing is up to date: is the world's dictionary still matched
one-to-one to the world's things — a noun for every creature, a verb for every
act?

The forward answer was reassuring. Every registered concept is carried across
three ledgers or is explicitly, reason-bearingly absent from one; the trial
balance foots; no entry in the dictionary lies. That machinery had been built
and had been working.

The reverse answer was that nobody had ever asked.

## The direction that was never built

A correspondence has two directions. *Does this concept have a word?* is one of
them, and it was thoroughly instrumented. *Does this thing have a concept?* is
the other, and exactly one instance of it existed — a line reporting phenomena
the world can emit that nothing names. Four sibling directions had never been
generalized, and behind one of them stood twelve creatures.

Treants, twig blights, giant elk, woolly mammoths, giant goats, an otyugh, a
xorn, a rust monster, three dragons, and an owlbear. Each simulated, each
placed on the map, each capable of walking up to a player and being described
to them — as *a wild treant*, rendered from its own internal key, because
there was no word to render instead. No concept, no dictionary row, no name in
any language of the world.

What makes this more than an oversight is *why* nothing caught it. The
correspondence machinery reconciles concepts against ledgers, so it can only
see a thing that has a concept. A creature with no concept has no row, and a
row is the only place an absence can be written down. The vocabulary could not
express the defect. Twelve creatures were not unnamed *in* the ledger; they
were invisible to it.

Git dates the break precisely. When concepts were introduced the world held
two species, and both were named. Two more peoples arrived later and were
named on arrival. Then a campaign authored twelve fauna, named none of them,
and said nothing about concepts in its spec or its chronicle — because nothing
asked it to. The invariant held for five days and broke in silence.

## The verbs had never been named at all

The other empty direction was worse in a quieter way. A creature in this world
plans: it forms an intention and executes an act — move, drink, rest, eat.
Those four acts are the vocabulary of everything any creature ever does. Three
of them had no concept.

And the one that did — `eat` — was filed as a *quality*, alongside `red` and
`two`, because the concept vocabulary had no category for a verb. The world's
acts were typed as properties. A creature could eat, and the dictionary
recorded eating as a kind of attribute one might have, like being yellow.

## Naming

Both directions now report, and neither reports from a list somebody
maintains. Orphan creatures are computed from the biosphere registry; orphan
acts from the planner's own action roster, which is held complete by a
compile-time tripwire — a fifth kind of act cannot be added to this world
without the compiler demanding to know its name first. Species cannot have
that guard, because its roster is data rather than code, so it leans on the
published page changing when the roster does.

Then the naming itself: twelve creatures given concepts, three acts given
concepts, and a new category — *act* — for the things a creature does, with
eating, sleeping, and dying moved into it from the properties where they had
been living.

The peoples got words. Goblin says **Neobea** for *drink*, hobgoblin
**Neogea**, bugbear **Doogoa** — three reflexes of one ancestral root,
inherited and weathered through each language's own sound changes, exactly as
their words for water and fire were. The twelve creatures came through as
gaps, and this is the right answer rather than a missing one: a gap here is
recountable, and each one recounts the same fact — no people in this world has
ever met a xorn. The word will exist when the meeting does.

And not one word already spoken changed. Every one of the three hundred and
twenty lines of the peoples' committed vocabulary is byte-identical; all
seventy-six ancestral roots survive untouched; the world's ledger is the same
seven thousand four hundred and sixty-six facts it was before. The world
gained fifteen names and lost nothing — which was only possible because
[The Accession](./the-accession.md), a campaign this one spawned by trying to
do the naming and discovering it could not be done cheaply, had made the
vocabulary appendable a day earlier.

## What the audit still cannot see

Three reverse directions remain. Two are merely unmeasured. The third is
named on the page as *unaudited*, deliberately and with its reason: the
player-facing prose carries a large authored vocabulary — relief descriptors,
sky and hydrology language, the words for how a creature feels — with no
concepts behind any of it. A room can be *a boulder field* or *buttressed
canopy*; a moon can be *smoldering red*; a creature *seems content*. None of
those are things the dictionary knows.

Auditing them is cheap. Knowing what to audit is not, because the line between
a thing that deserves a name and a texture that does not has never been drawn
here, and drawing it badly would either flood the vocabulary with scenery or
quietly bless the same silence this campaign spent its length removing. So the
page says so, in the same place it reports the gaps it *can* count. An
unaudited direction that announces itself is a different object from one
nobody has noticed.
