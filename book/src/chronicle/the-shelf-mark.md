# The Shelf-Mark

*Three entries in the catalogue had swallowed the cards behind them. The
call numbers were still printed at the top; the shelf they pointed to was
gone.*

A library catalogue works because a card is not the book. The card carries
just enough to recognize the work — a title, an author, a shelf-mark — and
then it points, and the pointing is the whole service. Let a card grow long
enough to hold the book's argument and it stops being a catalogue: you now
have two copies of the corpus, one of them worse, and the thing you go to
when you want to know whether a work exists has become the thing you have to
read to find out.

This project's index of speculative directions had been growing that way for
months, one campaign at a time, and nobody decided it. The convention was to
record a direction as a single line and point at wherever it was argued —
an essay, a spec, a chronicle. The convention held. What was never written
down is what happens *next*: a campaign ships something the line describes,
and the cheapest way to record that is to append a sentence saying so. Then
the next campaign appends its own. Nothing in that sequence is wrong at any
step, and the endpoint is an index with a mean entry of eight hundred
characters and a largest of twelve thousand — longer than most chapters of
the book it sits inside.

The question that opened this campaign was whether to delete the finished
entries, and the answer is no, emphatically: a finished entry is the most
valuable kind. An index of speculative directions earns its keep mostly by
saying *this was already tried* and *this was already decided*, and those
are exactly the entries a deletion pass would take. But the instinct behind
the question was reading something real. It had simply located it in the
wrong place — not in the presence of finished work, but in the length of
every entry regardless of status.

## The measurement that was wrong twice

The first count of over-long entries said two hundred and seventy-eight. The
real number is two hundred and thirty-seven, and the gap is instructive
because it is the same mistake made twice in one campaign, in two different
tools.

Entries live in a table whose columns are padded with trailing spaces so
they line up in the source. A measurement that does not trim that padding
counts the whitespace as prose, and forty-two entries that were already
perfectly short — one of them eighty characters long — were reported as
bloated. Earlier, a count of *structurally broken* entries said five; the
real number was three, because two of them escaped a special character
correctly and the counting tool did not model the escape.

Both errors have one shape: **a measuring tool that models the format
differently from the tool that enforces it will disagree with it, and the
disagreement is silent.** The repair was to stop measuring with a separate
script at all. The list of grandfathered entries is now generated by running
the enforcement check itself and reading its own complaint, so the two
cannot drift by construction.

## The three entries that had lost their shelf-mark

The sharpest finding was not about length.

A table row in this markup is split on a vertical bar. A vertical bar inside
a stretch of literal code is still a split — the code formatting does not
protect it — so three entries that mentioned a bar inside code had one more
column than the table has headings. The renderer's response is not to draw
an extra column. It truncates the row to the heading count, which shifts
every remaining column one place to the left and drops the last one off the
end.

The last column is the pointer.

So three entries had been published with their prose spilling into the
status column, their confidence rating showing where the pointer belongs,
and the pointer itself — the one thing this campaign argues an entry exists
to carry — silently deleted from the published page. Nothing noticed,
because every check the project had on this file was about whether its
*references resolved*, and none was about whether its *rows were shaped like
rows*. A missing reference fails loudly. A reference that was never rendered
at all fails silently.

Fixing the escaping fixed something else too. Of four entries whose status
had drifted outside the allowed vocabulary, three were not status bugs at
all: their status column was displaying prose because the columns had
shifted. One real defect had been presenting as four.

## Open where it should be open, closed where it should be closed

The check that reads this file already derived its category prefixes from
the file itself, deliberately, with a comment explaining why: coin a new
category and the check adapts instead of rotting. That reasoning is correct.
The category vocabulary is *open*.

The status vocabulary is not. There are six statuses, the file documents
them, and an entry outside that set is a defect rather than an innovation.
But nothing distinguished the two cases, so the status field was left open
by omission — and drifted, acquiring an invented seventh value that no
reader could interpret against the documented five. Naming the asymmetry was
most of the fix: derive what is open, hard-code what is closed, and say
which is which where the reader will look.

## A long entry is usually mislabelled

The rule the campaign settled on is not really about size.

The index already defines its own admission control and had simply never
enforced it. One status means *captured, not yet argued — a stub*. Another
means *has a full essay elsewhere*. Three hundred and twenty-three entries
claim the first, and they average six hundred and thirty-four characters of
argument.

So an over-long entry is not a long entry. It is a **mislabelled** one: an
idea carrying nine hundred characters of argument is not a stub, and by the
index's own rules its argument belongs in the essays with the entry pointing
at it. That reframing is what makes the limit principled rather than
cosmetic, and it is why no new holding pen was needed. Every over-long entry
resolves by one of three moves that were already legal: delete prose the
pointer already covers, move real argument to the essays and change the
status to match, or cut a wordy stub to a clause.

## The ratchet

Two hundred and thirty-seven entries could not be rewritten in one sitting,
and a limit that fails on arrival is a limit nobody can land. So it ships the
way this project has landed a strict rule against a large non-compliant
corpus before: the entries over the limit on the day it arrives are listed,
that list is exempt, and the list can only ever shrink. A new entry over the
limit fails. An entry that gets compacted must leave the list, and a
companion check fails if it does not — so the exemption cannot quietly
become permanent.

Forty-three entries were compacted here, the ones long enough to hurt
reading, taking the file down by seventy-seven kilobytes. The rest is a
burn-down that any later campaign can chip at, which is the entire reason the
ratchet exists. The index is not yet what it claims to be. It is now
prevented from getting further away.
