# The Threshold

*The Hearth* built a room's interior and then, deliberately, left it out of
reach. It could compose a fire, an alcove, a bed beside the fire; it could route
a creature from one to another; it could say how much warmth reached any of
them. What it could not do was any of that for a room that actually existed in
a world, because nothing derived an interior from a real place and no creature
had a position inside one. Every reading came back empty. That was the point —
it kept the campaign honest and the worlds unchanged.

This campaign crossed into it.

## What the crossing needed

Three things, and the first was a correction.

A room's interior had to be derived from the room itself: which patterns a
place draws, given whether anyone's territory it is and whether warmth matters
there. That last question turns out to be a property of the place rather than
of the day — a hearth must not appear and vanish with the seasons — so the
climate is read at one fixed reckoning and the interior is a pure function of
where you are. Not of when. There is a cost to that, stated where the constant
lives: a room that grows cold over long ages will never gain a fire, because
the reckoning never moves. It is invisible only because a life is short against
the drift of a climate.

A creature had to stand somewhere. Not stored — an entity's remembered position
is its room and nothing finer, and the finer position evaporates the moment
nobody is watching. That is the law, and it is the reason this layer can never
corrupt a world: what is not written cannot be wrong.

And a creature had to be able to *cross* — to move from the doorway to the fire
without that movement ever being mistaken for the coarser walk between rooms.

## The door that is not a door

The obvious model of arrival is that you enter a room through its threshold.
That is true of a house and false of a meadow. A field has no doorway; its whole
border is passable, and the exception outdoors — a ford, a col, a gap in a
cliff — is the *rule* indoors. Built and wild are mirror images of one another,
and a threshold is only one species of a wider thing: the seam where the two
scales of position meet.

Naming the genus exposed something the narrower name had hidden. Rooms sit on a
mesh with roughly six neighbours each, and the interior model gave each room a
single threshold — so a house with two doors had one doorway between them, and a
creature entering was placed at "the threshold" no matter which door it used.
The wilderness gap was the visible symptom; the error was general. A seam
belongs to the edge between two rooms, not to either room's furniture. Where
there is a chokepoint you arrive at it; where the whole border is open you
arrive at the heart of the place, because without coordinates there is no
nearer or further edge to speak of, and this world's rule is that outcomes read
shape, never distance.

## Four times asking, four times no

The campaign froze a prediction before it wrote a line: a cold creature with a
fire in its house should suffer the cold less than one without.

It does not. The measurement came back empty four times, and each emptiness
ruled out a different reason, which is the only way an empty result is worth
anything.

The first said the fire was too faint — a single degree at the hearth, an eighth
of a degree at the door. So the fire was recalculated from first principles: an
ordinary dwelling, its envelope leaking heat at a certain rate, a hearth burning
at a certain power, the radiant crowding near the flame that is why people sit
close. Fifteen degrees at the fire. The argument was written down and committed
before anything was measured again, so that the number could not be quietly
chosen to produce an answer.

Fifteen degrees changed nothing.

The third said the creature never reached the fire — it was reading warmth at
the door because that is where it had been put. So the creature was taught to
cross the room, and it did. Still nothing.

The fourth said the instrument was blind: the thing measuring these creatures
sampled a frozen moment that did not know where anyone had walked. So the
instrument was taught to look at where the creature actually stood, at the fire
itself, the whole fifteen degrees.

Still nothing. And with that, the reason is no longer a mystery but a fact about
the world: the creatures who live where it is cold are either comfortable —
their own tolerance is wider than the chill — or they are forty to eighty
degrees beyond any help a domestic fire could offer. There is no one in between
for a hearth to save. A fire is a great comfort to someone slightly too cold.
The world, as it stands, does not have those people indoors.

That is not a failure of the fire. It is a finding about who lives where.

## And then the ground moved

While all this was being settled, another hand was reshaping how a world's
history is laid down, and the place the measurement had been taken stopped
being the place it was. Where there had been ninety-two settlements there were
now a hundred and four.

On that new ground the fire does something. Very little — one creature in two
thousand feels the cold less for having one, and only among the hobgoblins —
but the little it does is in the direction that was predicted.

It would be easy, and wrong, to call that the answer. The prediction was sealed
against a world that no longer exists, and finding a favourable number
afterwards in a world that has changed is precisely the move the sealing was
meant to prevent. So it is written down and left unclaimed: the four empty
answers were honest measurements of the world as it was, and this is an
observation about the world as it now is, awaiting a measurement of its own.

What is claimed is narrower and safer: a fire never makes a creature colder.

## What was found by making something move

Twice, a thing that had been checked turned out to have been checked for the
wrong property.

A creature's walk across a room consulted which anchors lay *beside* one
another — and a fire is not beside its alcove, it is *within* it. So every
journey to the hearth stopped one step short, silently, and had done since the
step was written. Nothing detected it until a creature was actually asked to
make the journey. The remedy was to give the planner and the walker one shared
answer to what a single step is, rather than two agreeing answers that could
drift.

The validator that certifies a room walkable still held a third private copy of
that same answer — found last of all, in the final reading of the whole work,
by asking whether the consolidation had actually finished. It had not.

## What it is safe to say afterwards

The interior never persists, which is what makes it free to change. But a
creature's *position* being unwritten is not the same as its consequences being
unwritten: what a creature does because it was warm is remembered forever. So
the furnishing of rooms has quietly become one of the world's fixed
things — the set of patterns a place can draw is now load-bearing, and adding to
it is no longer a small matter but a change of era. That is the true price of
this crossing, and it was paid knowingly.

One habit is worth keeping. A creature that walks to the fire while unobserved
does not lose the walk when the world's attention returns: its own reasoning is
replayed forward from the last thing known about it, executing only what leaves
no trace, so that it is found where it would have been rather than back at the
door. Anything that would have marked the world is refused during that replay,
because a reconstruction that writes history is not a reconstruction.
