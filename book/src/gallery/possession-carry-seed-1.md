# A Possession of Seed 1 — a thing carried

*(This transcript is frozen. It is the only gallery page that types the
custody verbs — `take`, `drop`, `put`, `open`, `close`, `carrying` — and it
is the campaign's thesis end to end: a key is picked up beside the entrance
screen, carried three chambers into a hamlet dwelling, refused by a shut
lid, and then used to open that chest. Two refusals are the evidence, not
the noise. `take a key` one room in answers "You see no a key here." —
that room composes none — which is what makes the closing beat, where a key
set down in the front room is picked up again, a measurement rather than a
reply. And `take a key` at the shut chest answers "The key is shut away in
something closed.": the lid means something, and until this campaign's fix
round it did not.*

*Read the room descriptions as the GRAMMAR's catalogue and not as an
inventory, because that is what they are — and this dwelling has TWO keys,
which is what makes the difference visible. Chamber prose renders the
pattern the room was composed from, never the ledger, so it moves for
nobody: the front room says "a doorway, a screen and a key" on the last
entry, naming the key its grammar composes even though that key is by then
stowed in the chest three chambers away — while the key actually lying on
its floor is named by nothing (`PLAY-room-prose-omits-what-the-ledger-
holds`). The storeroom reads the same way in the other direction, listing
"a key" while the chest is shut on it
(`PLAY-closed-container-conceals-nothing`). Both are the same absent read,
and both are deferred with a priced bill rather than unnoticed.*

*One more thing not to mistake for a bug: `close` does not re-lock. A lid
and a lock are separate states, so the second `open` needs no key
(decision 0399).)*
